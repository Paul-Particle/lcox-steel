# CBAM indirect-emissions exclusion and imported "green iron": who has said what

Research date: 2026-09-08. Budget-constrained pass — priorities 1-4 covered well, 5 partially.

---

## 1. THE UK CONTRAST — RESOLVED. The premise needs retracting.

**The UK position CHANGED. It is not "included from the outset in 2027". It is deferred to 2029 at the earliest.**

### Original position (October 2024) — indirect emissions WERE to be included
HM Treasury / HMRC, *Introduction of a UK Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism from January 2027:
Government response to the policy design consultation*, October 2024.
https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/679cb194a9ee53687470a2fa/Introduction_of_a_UK_Carbon_Border_Adjustment_Mechanism_from_January_2027_-_Government_response_to_the_policy_design_consultation.pdf

- **Para 1.13:** "As previously announced, the UK CBAM will be applied to 'direct', 'indirect' and
  select 'precursor' product emissions embodied in imported CBAM goods, to ensure comparable
  coverage relative to the UK ETS."
- **Para 3.2 definition:** "Indirect emissions are emissions related to the production of
  electricity, which is consumed during the production of CBAM goods irrespective of whether the
  electricity was produced on or off site."
- **Para 3.10 (Government response):** "The government intends to proceed with the proposed
  definitions of direct and indirect emissions, including precursor emissions, for the
  implementation of the UK CBAM…"
- **Para 3.11 — the reasoning, verbatim:** "It is important for the UK CBAM to cover indirect
  emissions. This is to ensure that it places a price on the emissions embodied in imported
  products on a comparable basis to that which would have been payable had the goods been produced
  in the UK, where electricity emissions are subject to both the UK ETS and Carbon Price Support
  (CPS). In March 2022 the government extended the EII Compensation scheme to March 2025. HM
  Treasury and HMRC will continue to work with the Department for Business and Trade to ensure that
  the policy aims of the Compensation Scheme are reflected in the UK CBAM."
- **Para 3.74:** "As the UK ETS and CPS place a carbon price on indirect emissions, these costs
  will be reflected in the UK CBAM rate."

**Who objected — directly relevant to the EUROFER tension (Para 3.5):**
> "A small proportion of respondents were opposed to including indirect emissions in the UK CBAM,
> citing existing compensation for indirect carbon costs under the Energy Intensive Industry (EII)
> scheme and misalignment with the EU. **These responses came primarily from the aluminium and
> iron & steel industries.**"

So the *steel industry itself* lobbied the UK against including indirect emissions, on the same two
grounds the EU Commission uses (ICC/EII overlap, EU alignment).

### Reversal (26 November 2025, Budget 2025) — deferred to 2029
HM Treasury, *Budget 2025: Strong Foundations, Secure Future*, 26 November 2025.
https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6929b367b3b9afff34e9621c/Final_print_HMT_Budget_2025_TEXT_PRINT_NEW.pdf

- **Para 4.203, verbatim:** "Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) – The government will
  legislate in Finance Bill 2025-26 to introduce the CBAM from 1 January 2027. The inclusion of
  indirect emissions within scope of the CBAM will be delayed until 2029 at the earliest. **This is
  to reflect continued support for the Energy Intensive Industries (EII) Compensation Scheme.**"
  (Note: the "EII Compensation Scheme" sentence was added by an errata correction in the same
  document — the original print omitted the reason.)
- **Budget scorecard, line 69:** "Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM): Remove indirect
  emissions from scope of the CBAM from 1 January 2027" — Exchequer effect
  **+0m / −40m / −170m / −180m / −170m / −165m**. This is the fiscal price the UK put on the
  exclusion: roughly £165–180m/yr of forgone revenue. Useful as an order-of-magnitude analogue for
  the EU.
- The formal technical-consultation response was published **26 November 2025**; the stated
  reason in commentary is "concerns around the complexity and administrative challenges associated
  with reporting on indirect emissions" plus the EII overlap.

Secondary confirmations:
- https://taxscape.deloitte.com/measures-autumn-budget-2025/carbon-border-adjustment-mechanism.aspx
- https://www.hsfkramer.com/notes/energy/2026-posts/navigating-uk-cbam-policy-updates
- https://gmk.center/en/news/the-uk-cbam-will-not-include-indirect-emissions-until-2029/
- https://learn.carbonchain.io/en/articles/13015357-latest-on-uk-cbam
- https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/draft-legislation-carbon-border-adjustment-mechanism/outcome/draft-legislation-outcome-carbon-border-adjustment-mechanism

**Net effect on the gap:** the UK contrast has evaporated. As of 2027 both the EU and the UK price
only direct emissions for iron and steel. The UK actively *chose* the EU's position after
initially rejecting it, and named the EII/ICC overlap as the reason. The gap is now
EU-and-UK-wide, which strengthens rather than weakens the finding.

## 2. HAS THE IRON-SPECIFIC ARGUMENT BEEN MADE? YES — but thinly, and by academics and RMI, not by the EU policy NGOs.

### 2a. THE CLOSEST MATCH — Johnson, Åhman, Nilsson & Li, *Nature Communications*, 13 October 2025

"Emerging green steel markets surrounding the EU emissions trading system and carbon border
adjustment mechanism", Nature Communications, DOI 10.1038/s41467-025-64440-9. Authors: Constantin
Johnson, Max Åhman, Lars J. Nilsson, Zhenxi Li — Division of Environmental and Energy Systems
Studies, Lund University.
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-64440-9 | https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12518805/
| https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41083494/

This paper makes essentially the user's argument, explicitly and for iron:

> "Scope 2 emissions originating from electricity production are not included in the CBAM for iron,
> steel, and hydrogen due to World Trade Organization (WTO) compatibility and the conflict with the
> current European indirect cost compensation (ICC) scheme for electricity consumption."

> "**There is a risk of emission-intensive imports, such as HBI and H2-DRI-EAF steel, where hydrogen
> is produced by fossil electricity.**"

> "As electrification as a climate mitigation strategy shifts the current scope 1 emissions from
> combustion to scope 2, excluding scope 2 emissions in the CBAM means surrendering certain
> regulatory control of the industry transition."

> "The inclusion of scope 2 emissions further mitigates imported embedded emissions due to the
> higher grid-emission intensity of third countries compared to the EU27 average."

It also models the trade consequence: "Through the phase-out of free allocation and the phase-in of
the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), emission-intensive steel production and import is
replaced by more competitive low-emission secondary (scrap-EAF) and H2-DRI-EAF steel imports."

**Assessment:** this is the iron-specific argument in print, dated Oct 2025. It names HBI. It names
fossil-powered hydrogen. It names third-country grid intensity. What it does NOT do is quantify a
specific exporting grid (no Victoria/Tasmania-style contrast), and it is a modelling paper, not
advocacy — it has had no visible policy uptake. The user's contribution is the *measured,
grid-specific* version, not the qualitative point.

### 2b. RMI — the sharpest process-level statement, 28 August 2026

Quailan Homann and Rachel Wilmoth, *Understanding How Carbon and Trade Policies can Impact the
Steel Sector Transition*, RMI, 28 August 2026.
https://rmi.org/resources/understanding-how-carbon-and-trade-policies-can-impact-the-steel-sector-transition/

> "CBAM does not cover electricity associated with producing iron, steel, and its precursors
> (except electricity associated with producing agglomerated iron)."

RMI then enumerates exactly which electricity escapes pricing: **hydrogen production, iron ore
beneficiation, DRI pre-heating, and EAF use**. It also notes that grid-connected electrolysers in
fossil-dominated grids (it uses MENA as the example) would be exposed if electricity emissions were
included. This is the most precise public statement of the mechanism of the gap found in this
search — and it is only ~10 days old at time of writing.

Related RMI: https://rmi.org/resources/how-better-policy-can-support-japans-green-steel-opportunity/

### 2c. Sandbag — makes the *hydrogen* version, not the *iron* version

Adrien Assous, *Extending the CBAM to indirect emissions*, Sandbag, brief dated July 2025
(published 1 Aug 2025).
https://sandbag.be/2025/08/01/why-the-cbam-should-cover-indirect-emissions/
PDF: https://sandbag.be/wp-content/uploads/2025.07.30-ICC-and-CBAM-indirect-emissions.pdf

Verbatim, the environmental-integrity argument:
> "The current exclusion of indirect emissions covered by the CBAM fails to price an important
> source of CO2 emissions in imported goods, which is environmentally weak. For example, **hydrogen
> can help reduce direct emissions from industries like steelmaking, but if it is made from
> high-emission electricity, it could emit more CO2 than steel from blast furnaces.** In addition,
> it fails to incentivise third countries to decarbonise their electricity systems."

And the future-proofing argument, which is the nearest Sandbag gets to the arbitrage framing:
> "As electrification accelerates and carbon prices continue to rise, indirect emissions will
> account for a growing share of industrial emissions. EU producers will face more indirect costs
> due to electrification and higher carbon prices, while **importers will use more electricity to
> exploit the lack of coverage of indirect emissions in the CBAM.** Indirect emissions should
> therefore be covered by the CBAM as soon as possible."

Figures (verify against the user's recollection — these differ):
- "The ICC scheme cost **€600m to France and €1.6bn to Germany in 2023**." (The user had €2.4bn for
  Germany; the brief says €1.6bn for 2023. The €2.4bn figure is not in this brief.)
- "only **14 Member States** participate in the Indirect Cost Compensation (ICC) scheme. For
  installations located in the remaining 16 countries, the exclusion of indirect emissions from the
  CBAM therefore creates unfair competition with imports from overseas."

Sandbag's second recommendation is an ICC reform, not just CBAM extension: "Reform ICC by limiting
compensation to non-fossil electricity and periods when fossil fuels set the marginal price."
Rationale: "Currently, all electricity consumed by the industry is eligible for compensation,
including fossil electricity, which provides no incentive for consumers to source low-carbon
electricity. Industrial installations using 24-hour grid electricity are rewarded all the same."

**Key negative finding: Sandbag's brief contains no mention of DRI, HBI, sponge iron or green iron.**
The steelmaking example is hydrogen-in-the-EU, not iron-imported-into-the-EU. The argument is
framed as competitive fairness for EU producers plus fiscal relief for Member States — not as an
import loophole for electro-iron.

### 2d. Sandbag Algeria (20 February 2026) — adjacent but does not close the loop
https://sandbag.be/2026/02/20/eu-cbam-algeria-iron-exports/

- Algeria exported ~350kt long steel and **230kt DRI** to the EU in 2024.
- "Since Algerian DRI has much lower emissions than European pig iron, which currently receives a
  large number of free allowances, the CBAM should create a **net profit of €54 per tonne of DRI**,
  and **€12 million** for the 350kt."
- A shift of 350kt from long steel to DRI would turn "a €29m net loss into a €19m net gain."

This is the *free-allocation* asymmetry, not the indirect-emissions exclusion. Algerian DRI is
gas-based, so the electricity point does not bind there. **Sandbag does not connect its own
indirect-emissions brief to its own DRI analysis.** The two sit in separate publications with no
cross-reference. That non-connection is itself a finding.

### 2g. Agora Industry — flags the PPA/direct-link zeroing problem, which is the adjacent gap
*The role of green iron trade in accelerating competitive steel transformation*, Agora Industry.
https://www.agora-industry.org/publications/the-role-of-green-iron-trade-in-accelerating-steel-transformation
(also https://www.agora-industry.org/publications/achieving-climate-neutral-steel-by-2050)

Agora is pro-import: "Reinforcing the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) can help secure
downstream steel activities, and accessing lower-cost green iron via strategic partnerships with
green HBI producers can serve as a competitiveness factor for the European steel industry."

But it identifies a real verification hole in how indirect emissions *would* be counted:
> "Under the CBAM, emissions attributed to electricity use in manufacturing are based on either the
> country's grid emission factor or, if the manufacturing plant has a direct link or power purchase
> agreement with a renewable energy source, zero. However, **this allows third-country exporters to
> reduce their CBAM fees by signing PPAs or connecting to renewable energy without necessarily
> benefitting the climate.**"

This matters for the user's argument: even if indirect emissions *were* included for iron, the
direct-link/PPA rule would let a Victorian project zero out its 781 gCO2eq/kWh grid on paper. That
is a second-order gap the user should anticipate as the obvious rebuttal.

### 2i. Verdict on the key question
The **general** scope 2 argument is well established (Sandbag, Bellona, EUROFER-for-ferroalloys,
the Commission's own 2027 review clause). The **iron-specific** argument — that imported
electro-iron carries near-zero embedded emissions regardless of the grid — has been made:
- qualitatively and with HBI named, by **Johnson/Åhman/Nilsson/Li in Nature Communications,
  13 Oct 2025**;
- mechanistically and process-by-process, by **RMI, 28 Aug 2026**.
It has **not** been made by any EU policy NGO, by any trade association, or in the trade press with
a named exporting-grid figure. **No one has published a measured grid-intensity contrast** (the
Victoria 781 vs Tasmania 10 gCO2eq/kWh framing). That specific, quantified version appears
genuinely unremarked.

## 3. EU STEEL INDUSTRY — EUROFER OPPOSES inclusion for steelmaking. The tension is explicit and published.

### EUROFER's actual position, verbatim
EUROFER, *Fix that leak! — CBAM factsheet*
https://www.eurofer.eu/assets/publications/brochures-booklets-and-factsheets/fix-that-leak/CBAM-Facsheet-EUROFER.pdf
(campaign page: https://www.eurofer.eu/publications/brochures-booklets-and-factsheets/fix-that-leak)

The factsheet's own summary bullet:
> "**INDIRECT EMISSIONS: Maintain indirect cost compensation for steelmaking and include indirect
> emissions of ferro-alloys in the CBAM.**"

The section is literally headed:
> "**Indirect emissions under CBAM: no for steelmaking, yes for ferro alloys**"

The reasoning for opposing inclusion for steelmaking:
> "Under the current electricity market design, prices are based on marginal pricing—meaning the
> cost of electricity is set by the most expensive energy source (often natural gas or coal). Even
> if a steel company uses clean electricity, it still pays the carbon cost of fossil-based power
> generation due to this pricing model. The current carbon leakage instrument for indirect emissions
> (indirect costs compensation) takes into account this pricing mechanism. Therefore, indirect costs
> compensation for steelmaking should be maintained and implemented in all member states."
(Supporting datapoint in the factsheet: "86% of the times fossil fuels set electricity prices" in
2022, projected ~80% in 2030.)

The carve-out they DO want:
> "Instead, ferro-alloys have a different, unique situation. Firstly, they are not produced in the
> EU (ferro-nickel and nickel-pig iron), or to a very limited extent (ferro-chromium). Secondly,
> they have substantially higher embedded emissions in imported goods compared to EU production. In
> particular, EU stainless steel producers source most of their ferro-alloys from scrap, while
> ferro-alloys embedded in imported stainless steel come mainly from virgin materials. Therefore,
> indirect emissions of ferro-alloys (as CBAM goods and as stainless steel precursors) should be
> included in the CBAM."

**This is the answer to the user's tension hypothesis, and it is stronger than hypothesised.**
EUROFER does not merely decline to push for indirect inclusion for iron and steel — it explicitly
argues AGAINST it, and asks for inclusion only in the one precursor category (ferro-alloys) where
EU producers are net buyers rather than net makers. The pattern is exactly self-interested: include
indirect emissions where we import the input, exclude them where we make the product. Imported HBI
falls in the "we want to import it" bucket, so the exclusion suits them.

Corroboration of the same position from a secondary source:
https://gmk.center/en/news/eurofer-calls-for-closing-gaps-in-cbam/
> "EUROFER calls for retaining compensation for indirect costs of steelmaking and including
> indirect emissions for ferroalloys in the CBAM."

### EUROFER's other CBAM asks (no indirect-emissions ask among them)
*The CBAM must be fixed and launched urgently*, EUROFER, **22 January 2025**
https://www.eurofer.eu/publications/position-papers/the-cbam-must-be-fixed-and-launched-urgently

remain competitive globally"; (2) "Implementing stricter rules against circumvention practices,

Also relevant:

- https://www.eurofer.eu/assets/Uploads/20210927-EUROFER-comments-CBAM.pdf (2021 comments)

### The steel industry told the UK the same thing

## 4. HBI/DRI IMPORT PLANS OF EU STEELMAKERS

Headline: **EU steelmakers are cancelling domestic DRI and buying imported HBI instead.** No
company found is publicly relying on the CBAM indirect exclusion in its own words, and no company
found is publicly committing to a verified renewable-power carbon intensity for imported iron.

### ArcelorMittal — retreat from EU DRI, retention of North/South American DRI

### A live example of low-direct-emission steel entering the EU
**China's HBIS Group, July 2025**: first commercial contract to export **10,000 t of
hydrogen-reduced steel to Italy**, produced on Danieli's Energiron DRI platform, with a claimed 50%
lower carbon footprint than BF-BOF, accompanied by an audited EPD. Per Climate Energy Finance
(30 Apr 2026, p.66): "the monitoring, reporting and verification of embodied emissions of the
shipment and production facility mean the exports to Italy would meet CBAM compliance criteria,
significantly lowering the border carbon adjustment penalty relative to the default emissions value
placed upon Chinese iron and steel exports."
Original: FuelCellsWorks, "China's HBIS to Export 10,000 Tonnes of Hydrogen-Reduced Green Steel to
Italy", 25 July 2025.
**China's grid is ~500-550 gCO2eq/kWh. No indirect emissions were priced on this shipment.** This is
arguably the first real-world instance of the gap being used, and CEF reports it approvingly as a
CBAM success.

### The "Green HBI Trade" framing
Multiple trade sources describe an emerging model in which "Europe will increasingly import green
iron to feed its mills and comply with regulations like the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism
(CBAM)" and in which "importing hot-briquetted iron (HBI) and DRI from origins such as the
Middle East-North Africa region (MENA), where HBI/DRI production is more commercially viable" is a
leading scenario. Note the phrasing: importing HBI is presented as a route to *CBAM compliance*.
That is the demand-side mirror of the user's argument, and it is being said openly — but framed as
a virtue, not a loophole.

## 5. THE COUNTER-ARGUMENT — there is a real one, and it is the Commission's own.

### The official reasoning (trade press, primary quote)
Laura Roberts, "EU set to keep indirect emissions out of CBAM for metals; adds pre-consumer scrap
as separate product", **Fastmarkets, 7 November 2025**
https://www.fastmarkets.com/insights/eu-to-keep-indirect-emissions-out-of-cbam-for-metals/

**Martin Becker, deputy head of unit, European Commission**, quoted:
> "Our main concern at the moment is metals, both steel and aluminium, that are both CBAM products
> where indirect emissions are not in scope."

Becker also noted that using default values poses "major problems from a [World Trade Organization]
angle" — a WTO objection to the *mechanism* for pricing indirect emissions (country default grid
factors), which is the crux of the verification defence.

**Industry support for the exclusion:** Norsk Hydro welcomed the decision, arguing inclusion would
likely eliminate ETS cost compensation for indirect carbon costs in power prices, with Norwegian
smelters facing ~**$270/t** additional costs by 2035.

### The four defences, assembled
1. **ICC/EII overlap (strongest).** EU and UK producers already receive indirect cost compensation
   because marginal pricing makes them pay fossil carbon costs in their power bills even when
   consuming clean electricity. Charging importers for indirect emissions while compensating
   domestic producers for them is arguably double-dipping. EUROFER's marginal-pricing argument
   (§3) is a genuine economic point, not just rent-seeking. Sandbag concedes it: "integrating
   indirect emissions into the CBAM cannot be done with ICC in place in its current form."
   The UK named this explicitly as its reason for deferral (Budget 2025 para 4.203).
2. **WTO compatibility.** Default country grid factors risk being found discriminatory; per Becker,
   defaults pose "major problems from a WTO angle". The Nature Comms paper cites WTO compatibility
   as one of the two stated reasons for the exclusion.
3. **Verification of a third-country grid factor.** The UK's own reason for deferral was
   "complexity and administrative challenges associated with reporting on indirect emissions".
   Contested electricity accounting: which factor — national average, marginal, hourly, PPA-matched?
4. **Risk of penalising electrification.** Including scope 2 raises the border price of exactly the
   electro-intensive technologies (H2-DRI, MOE, electrowinning) the EU wants scaled. This is the
   defence most directly in tension with the user's argument.

### Does anyone address the green-iron case *within* the defence? NO.
No source located argues "the exclusion is correct *even though* it lets dirty-grid green iron in
free". The defence is entirely about ICC overlap, WTO risk and admin burden. The green-iron
consequence is not acknowledged by defenders of the status quo. **This is an unoccupied position in
the debate — the strongest reason the user's finding is publishable.**

### The rebuttal the user should pre-empt
Agora's PPA/direct-link point (§2g): even with indirect emissions included, CBAM's rule zeroes
electricity emissions where there is "a direct link or power purchase agreement with a renewable
energy source", which "allows third-country exporters to reduce their CBAM fees by signing PPAs or
connecting to renewable energy without necessarily benefitting the climate." A Victorian project on
a 781 g grid with a paper PPA would report zero. Extending scope to indirect emissions is therefore
necessary but not sufficient; the user needs an additionality/temporal-matching argument too (i.e.
the RFNBO rules the user is already comparing against).

### Ongoing EU process — the door is open
- **Technical Study on Indirect Emissions in the CBAM**, DG TAXUD, published **8 June 2026**
  https://taxation-customs.ec.europa.eu/news/technical-study-indirect-emissions-cbam-2026-06-08_en
  Three tasks: (1) setting default emission factors for indirect emissions; (2) conditions under
  which declarants may claim actual indirect emissions — "direct technical links, power purchase
  agreements (PPAs), and verification"; (3) feasibility of extending indirect coverage beyond
  current CBAM sectors. Five publication components including a final report, via the EU
  Publications Office. **This is the live policy window and the most important document to read
  next — not yet read in this pass.**
- The Commission's CBAM Review Report and the Dec 2025 amendment package: a **report in 2027** will
  "evaluate ways to extend the scope further to indirect emissions from CBAM goods (iron and steel,
  aluminium and hydrogen)". So the EU has a statutory 2027 review of exactly this question.
