# Is the CBAM indirect-emissions gap for imported green iron KNOWN, ACCEPTED, INTENDED?

Research date: 8 September 2026. Sources are EU institutional documents where obtainable.

**Throughout, two distinct claims are separated:**
- **(i)** the *general* point: indirect (scope 2 / electricity) emissions are excluded from CBAM for iron & steel, aluminium and hydrogen.
- **(ii)** the *specific* point: iron made abroad by H2-DRI or electrolysis has near-zero **direct** emissions, so it owes little or no CBAM **even if the hydrogen/electricity behind it is fossil-fired** — i.e. the gap is largest exactly for the product the EU most wants to import.

**Headline: (i) is exhaustively documented by the institutions. (ii) is, on this evidence, absent from the entire institutional record.**

## 1. THE REVIEW MANDATE — Article 30

### 1.1 Which mandate, and was it discharged?

The mandate is **Article 30(2)** of Regulation (EU) 2023/956, due **before the end of the transitional period** (i.e. before 31 December 2025). The Commission's own contractor describes it thus (Technical Study, §1, p. 24):

> "During the definitive period, indirect emissions obligations apply only to a subset of CBAM goods (cement; fertilisers and agglomerated iron ores) while the Regulation requires the Commission to report on the possibility of extending indirect emissions coverage to additional goods before the end of the transitional period."

This is confirmed from the Commission side in the explanatory memorandum to **COM(2025) 989** (ST-16973-2025, p. 2):

> "The Commission also conducted a broad review of CBAM in line with Article 30(2) of the CBAM Regulation. This review took stock of how the mechanism has worked so far, assessing relations with and impacts on developing countries including least developed countries (LDCs), and considers possible next steps. The review also considered the possibility for future extension of the CBAM to other EU ETS sectors at risk of carbon leakage, as well as downstream products in other sectors (cement, fertilisers and hydrogen)."

and (ST-16973-2025, p. ~14, "Implementation plans and monitoring, evaluation and reporting arrangements"):

> "Before the end of the transitional period, and in line with Article 30(2) of the CBAM Regulation, the Commission is to report to the European Parliament and the Council with a comprehensive review report on the lessons learnt from the transitional period. This report will also outline the possible future steps for further revisions and scope extensions."

**The Article 30(2) deliverable is COM(2025) 783 final, 16 December 2025.** So the mandate has been *discharged* — and discharged with a decision *not* to extend. Classification: **(i)**.

### 1.2 Article 30(3) — the downstream mandate, which *was* acted on

Contrast is instructive. ST-16973-2025, p. 1:

> "Acknowledging this risk, Article 30(3) of the CBAM Regulation requires the Commission to identify downstream products at risk of carbon leakage for possible inclusion in the scope of CBAM."

Article 30(3) produced a legislative proposal within months. Article 30(2)'s indirect-emissions limb produced a report that declined to act. Same regulation, same review cycle, opposite outcomes — this is a *choice*, not an oversight.

### 1.3 The "2027 report" — status: a soft, secondary-sourced promise, NOT an Article 30(2) obligation

Two secondary sources state a 2027 follow-up:

> "In 2027, the Commission plans to publish a report evaluating ways to extend the CBAM scope to indirect emissions from further CBAM goods (iron and steel, aluminium and hydrogen)."
> — European Hydrogen Observatory / CBAM guidance summaries

> "A report will follow in 2027 with an evaluation of ways to extend the scope to indirect emissions from further CBAM goods including iron and steel, aluminium and hydrogen."

I could **not** locate an Article of Regulation 2023/956 that mandates a *2027* report specifically on indirect emissions. What *is* in the amended text is the **biennial Article 30(6) report**, and its content list is where the significance lies (see 1.4). Treat "the 2027 report" as a Commission *intention* announced in COM(2025) 783, not a statutory deadline. **Flagging this as the weakest link in the user's prior brief** — it should not be cited as a legal obligation without seeing COM(2025) 783 §5.1 directly.

### 1.4 Article 30(6) as amended — indirect emissions are NOT in the reporting list

This is a **primary-source finding**, verbatim from the Council's June 2026 text and the Dec-2025 proposal (ST-16973-2025, Art. 1(20), p. 28):

> "(20) in Article 30(6), the second subparagraph is replaced by the following:
> 'Before 1 January 2028, as well as every two years thereafter, the Commission shall present a report to the European Parliament and to the Council on the application of this Regulation and functioning of the CBAM. This report may, where appropriate, be accompanied by a legislative proposal or implementing or delegated acts adopted pursuant to this Regulation. The report shall contain at least the following:
> (a) an assessment of the impact of the CBAM on:
> (i) carbon leakage, including in relation to exports;
> (ii) the sectors covered;
> (iii) internal market, economic and territorial impact throughout the Union;
> (iv) inflation and the price of commodities;
> (v) the effect on industries using goods listed in Annex I;
> (vi) international trade, including resource shuffling; and
> (vii) LDCs.'"

**Confirmed:** resource shuffling is filed under **Article 30(6)(a)(vi), "international trade"** — exactly as the user had it. And **indirect emissions appear nowhere in the mandatory content list.** The recurring statutory report the EU has just legislated for itself does not require it to revisit the indirect-emissions gap. Classification: **(i)**, and a strong "accepted" signal.

### 1.5 The link to ETS Indirect Cost Compensation (ICC) — explicit and load-bearing

Recital 19 of Reg. 2023/956 ties the Annex II carve-out to ICC ("financial measures … that compensate for indirect emissions costs"). The Commission's own study makes the linkage the organising principle of its entire Task 3, and confirms ICC reform is the gating factor. Technical Study, Task 3 conclusion:

> "For potential scope extension (Task 3), stakeholder evidence confirmed that the limited coverage of indirect emissions is widely perceived as insufficient to address carbon leakage in electricity-intensive sectors. However, immediate full extension without careful alignment with Indirect Cost Compensation would create risks of double protection, uneven treatment, and implementation complexity. Phased or ICC-linked approaches appear more workable, though they increase administrative coordination requirements."

And the Council's June 2026 general approach quietly *protects* ICC entitlement even for temporarily exempted goods (ST-10423-2026, Art. 1, p. 49):

> "During the period of validity of the exemption, the reduction of free allocation under Article 10a of Directive 2003/87/EC shall not apply to installations covered by that Directive for the share of their production covered by the exempted goods. These installations shall not be prevented from receiving, where applicable, compensation for indirect emission costs."

So: the ICC linkage is not merely rhetorical cover. It is a live legal entitlement the Council is actively ring-fencing. Answering "how explicitly is it linked?" — **very explicitly, and in operative text, not just recitals.** Classification: **(i)**.

### 3.1 The concession, verbatim

Executive summary, Task 3, p. 12:

> "The analysis confirmed that excluding indirect emissions from CBAM creates a structural inconsistency in carbon cost treatment in emission-intensive sectors. EU producers face indirect carbon costs through higher electricity prices reflecting the EU ETS, even where ICC compensates part of these costs. Third-country operators in most CBAM sectors do not face an equivalent obligation. This results in uneven carbon cost treatment across sectors in electricity-intensive value chains. From a purely environmental and carbon leakage perspective, applying CBAM to indirect emissions for all CBAM goods (except electricity) could be more effective than maintaining the selective approach."

On the "full coverage" option, p. 12:

> "Configurations that apply full CBAM coverage to indirect emissions (i.e. for all CBAM goods except electricity) perform best on environmental integrity. They ensure that all electricity-related emissions embedded in imports are subject to a carbon price, closing sectoral gaps and strengthening the decarbonisation signal."

On why it is nevertheless not done — the double-protection bind, p. 12:

> "However, if full CBAM coverage is introduced while ICC continues unchanged, the approach does not meet the even-handed treatment criterion. EU producers could continue to receive compensation for indirect carbon costs while importers are charged CBAM for the same emissions. This creates a clear risk of double protection and weakens the defensibility of the system. The practical finding was therefore that full CBAM coverage combined with unchanged ICC is environmentally strong but structurally misaligned and difficult to justify."

On removing ICC, p. 12:

> "Fully removing ICC entirely at the moment when full CBAM coverage could be introduced resolves this overlap. In that configuration, environmental integrity remains strong, even-handedness is restored, and the system is administratively simple. From a design perspective, this is one of the most internally coherent models. However, the analysis found that it scores worst on ease of adaptation. … The conclusion was that while this model is conceptually sound and logically consistent, it implies a more abrupt cost redistribution and therefore carries higher political and transition risks."

The study also confirms the sequencing that most resembles the present state of affairs is the *worst* option, p. 13:

> "A sequencing approach in which ICC is fully phased out before CBAM coverage of indirect emissions is introduced performs relatively poorly in environmental terms during the transition. Indirect emissions remain unpriced at the border for an extended period, reducing carbon leakage protection precisely while compensation is being withdrawn."

Study's own definition of the risk it is tracking, p. 148 (glossary):

> "Resource shuffling: Reallocation of existing low-carbon electricity (or its attributes) to the [production of CBAM goods exported to the EU]"

and p. 429:

> "Resource shuffling can increase the risk of carbon leakage without providing meaningful [emissions reductions] … Resource shuffling is identified as the most relevant risk for maintaining CBAM's environmental integrity."

**So the gap is KNOWN in the strongest possible sense — the Commission paid for a multi-hundred-page study of it, and the study says the exclusion is a "structural inconsistency" and a leakage risk. Verified. Classification: (i).**

The Commission's own dedicated, exhaustive study of the indirect-emissions gap in CBAM — the one document in the entire EU institutional apparatus whose sole purpose is to analyse this exact problem — **never once mentions direct reduced iron, HBI, sponge iron, green iron, electrolytic iron, or hydrogen-based iron.**

## 4. THE ONE PLACE THE COMMISSION DID ENGAGE THE DRI PRODUCT CASE — Implementing Reg. 2025/2620

**Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2620 of 16 December 2025**, recital 16. Substance verified via search summary of the OJ text (OJ text itself 403'd):

> "direct reduced iron (DRI) is covered by the hot metal benchmark of the EU ETS and, without further differentiation, imports of steel based on natural-gas DRI would receive a free allocation adjustment that exceeds their embedded emissions for the first years in which a CBAM obligation is due, which means that **no CBAM certificates would be due for DRI-based goods**."

> "To ensure the environmental integrity of the CBAM, the act therefore establishes a dedicated CBAM benchmark for natural gas-based DRI."

> "CBAM benchmarks per CBAM product can also differ based on the production route such as for natural gas-based direct-reduced iron (DRI), as well as electric arc furnace (EAF) produced steel."

URLs:
- https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=OJ:L_202502620
- https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_impl/2025/2620/oj/eng
- Commentary: https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/12/european-commission-issues-cbam-operational-rules-and-proposes-downstream-extension-of-the-cbam-scope

**Interpretation — this is the single most important source for the user's thesis, and it cuts both ways.**

FOR "known/intended": The Commission demonstrably *did* notice the specific product case. It spotted that a naive mirror of ETS free allocation would zero out CBAM liability for DRI-based goods, called that a threat to "environmental integrity", and legislated a bespoke lower benchmark to stop it. So the Commission is **not** blind to DRI as a special CBAM case, and it is willing to act via implementing act when it sees a zero-liability outcome.

AGAINST: **the problem it fixed is the *direct*-emissions benchmark problem, not the indirect-emissions problem.** Recital 16 is about *gas-based* DRI, whose direct emissions are real and simply mis-benchmarked. It says nothing about *hydrogen-based or electrolytic* DRI, whose direct emissions are genuinely near zero and whose electricity emissions are excluded by Annex II. The Commission closed the arithmetic loophole and left the structural one open.

The user's benchmark figures (0.295 / 0.397 tCO2e/t) are consistent with a dedicated gas-DRI benchmark but I could **not** verify those exact numbers against the OJ Annex; cite them only after checking the Annex directly. Classification: **(ii)** in framing, **but only for gas-based DRI** — the fossil-electricity/H2 case remains unaddressed.

### 5.5 But the Action Plan *does* flag the electricity-attribute problem — the closest institutional near-miss

> "Third, there is a risk of circumvention of CBAM objectives when goods produced in low-carbon production facilities in third countries are redirected to European customers while carbon-intensive production continues for other markets (so-called 'shuffling'). Similarly, greenwashing can occur through carbon accounting practices, such as **energy-intensive metals production benefiting from market-based instruments to appear low-carbon while still relying on high-emission energy sources** or misleading scrap content accounting practices."

That bolded clause is the nearest the Commission comes to point (ii) in a strategy document: production that *looks* low-carbon via electricity attribute purchases while actually running on high-emission power. But note the framing — it is filed as **greenwashing/circumvention to be policed**, not as a **structural consequence of the Annex II carve-out**. And it is aimed at metals generally, not at imported iron specifically. The Commission treats it as an enforcement problem, not a scope problem.

### 6.2 Resource shuffling is now DEFINED IN COUNCIL TEXT — and it is product shuffling, not electricity shuffling

This is a significant update on the user's prior (which had a statutory definition only in Parliament's ENVI report). **The Council inserted it too.** New recital (12a), ST-10423-2026, p. 8, verbatim:

> "(12a) Abusive practices include, but are not limited to, practices of harmful resource shuffling, where actors, controlling several production sites, with different technologies of production with material differences in emission intensity purposefully export to the Union the cleanest proportion of the goods without engaging in any structural decarbonisation strategy for the rest of the production of those goods, while not being exposed to a carbon price comparable to that applicable to production of the same goods in the Union. Due to harmful resource shuffling, imported goods could make Union producers' operations unviable. The subsequent emissions reduction in the Union due to disappearance of EU production would be offset by an increase in emissions in the rest of the world, if the demand for these goods in the Union would be covered by third-country production, meaning carbon leakage occurs. Therefore, harmful resource shuffling risks undermining the effectiveness of the CBAM in addressing the risk of carbon leakage in the Union and the attainment of the Union's climate policy objectives."

New recital (12b), p. 8–9 (mechanism — reverse burden of proof on a CN-code/origin subset):

> "(12b) Given the importance and urgency of addressing the risk of abusive practices in the form of harmful resource shuffling, the Commission should do its utmost to act by way of implementing acts as soon as possible after entry into force of this regulation. The risk of harmful resource shuffling can be circumscribed to a subset of CN codes and origins of the goods listed in Annex I to Regulation (EU) 2023/956, for which a high likelihood of harmful resource shuffling occurring can be demonstrated. This risk can be appreciated by reference to the level of the climate ambition of a third country, the heterogeneity of emissions, at country-level, embedded in products imported into the EU, the volume of goods imported into the Union from a third-country, as well as the economic incentives for operators to engage in harmful resource shuffling. For this subset of CN codes and origins, it is justified to introduce by way of implementing acts conditions to be fulfilled by operators and to request evidence to be provided by the CBAM declarant, demonstrating that no harmful resource shuffling has occurred … Where the evidence provided by the CBAM declarant is insufficient to demonstrate that no harmful resource shuffling has occurred, the CBAM declaration would have to be based on the default values as determined by the Commission."

New recital (12c), p. 9 (monitoring, and the Expert Group reference):

> "(12c) The Commission should monitor and assess the evolution of the risk of abusive practices, including harmful resource shuffling, for combinations of goods and third countries and swiftly update the list of goods for which additional evidence is required for the determination of the embedded emissions on the basis of actual emissions. The Commission should conduct its monitoring through an analysis of customs import declarations and CBAM declarations or on the basis of any relevant source of information, including from Member States through exchanges in the **Expert Group on the CBAM** or other relevant exchanges."

**Critical analytical point.** The Council's definition of resource shuffling is about **shuffling finished goods** — sending your cleanest tonnes to the EU. It is *not* about **shuffling electricity attributes**, which is what the Technical Study's glossary defines resource shuffling to mean ("reallocation of existing low-carbon electricity (or its attributes)"). **The two EU institutions are using the same term for two different phenomena.** The Council's version cannot catch the imported-green-iron case at all: a producer who builds a genuinely new H2-DRI plant abroad and ships all of its output to the EU is not shuffling anything under the Council's definition, yet carries zero CBAM liability for the fossil power behind its hydrogen. Classification: the Council's text is **(ii)-adjacent but structurally incapable of reaching (ii)**.

## 11. VERDICT

### KNOWN — yes, unambiguously, but only as (i)

Strongest evidence: the Commission commissioned and published a dedicated study whose Task 3 exists solely to analyse this gap, and which states that "excluding indirect emissions from CBAM creates a structural inconsistency in carbon cost treatment in emission-intensive sectors" and that full coverage "could be more effective than maintaining the selective approach". A DG TAXUD deputy head of unit said on the record that metals are "our main concern at the moment". This is not an oversight; it is a documented, studied, named problem.

**But (ii) is not known.** The Commission's own exhaustive study of the gap never mentions DRI, HBI, sponge iron, green iron, electrolytic iron or hydrogen-based iron — not once in 7,217 lines. Nor does the Steel and Metals Action Plan. Nor does the Council's general approach (which lacks even the word "hydrogen"). Nor does the leading NGO critique. The institutions know the sectoral carve-off; they have not analysed the product case where the carve-out bites hardest.

### ACCEPTED — yes, deliberately and repeatedly, as (i)

Strongest evidence, in ascending order of force:
1. Becker, 30 Oct 2025: "There is no intention in December to make a proposal to extend the scope to indirect emissions for these two sectors."
2. COM(2025) 989 delivers on that: three mentions of indirect emissions in 5,427 lines, all about default values for the already-covered sectors.
3. The Article 30(2) mandate — the one legal hook requiring a report on extension — was **discharged in December 2025 with a decision not to act**, while the *sibling* mandate in Article 30(3) produced a legislative proposal in the same package. Same review, same month: act on downstream scope, decline on indirect emissions.
4. **The decisive one:** the newly legislated Article 30(6) biennial report (before 1 Jan 2028, then every two years) enumerates seven mandatory assessment topics — carbon leakage, sectors covered, internal market, inflation, downstream industries, international trade including resource shuffling, LDCs — and **indirect emissions is not among them.** The EU has just written its own recurring homework and left this off the list.
5. The Council is actively *protecting* the ICC entitlement that is the carve-out's stated justification, in operative text: "These installations shall not be prevented from receiving, where applicable, compensation for indirect emission costs."

Acceptance is coherent, not lazy: the study shows every fix requires touching ICC/State aid, and the option that resolves the overlap cleanly "scores worst on ease of adaptation" with "higher political and transition risks". The EU has chosen the double-protection-avoidance branch and is content there. Both institutions' preferred remedy is instead **resource shuffling enforcement** — and the Council's definition of resource shuffling is about shipping your cleanest *goods*, which structurally cannot reach a genuinely new H2-DRI plant abroad running on fossil power.

### INTENDED — no. Importing green iron is not an EU objective, and the gap is a by-product, not a design

Strongest evidence: the flagship COM(2025) 125 contains **zero** occurrences of "green iron", "iron ore", "hot briquetted iron", "sponge iron" or "direct reduced iron". Its Section 3 is titled "PROMOTING AND PROTECTING EUROPEAN INDUSTRIAL CAPACITIES" and laments that "The EU is the only major steelmaking region seeing a decrease in capacity". Its treatment of H2-DR is as a **domestic** route to be fed by imported hydrogen molecules and Hydrogen Bank support — the Commission wants to import *hydrogen*, not iron. JRC concurs, framing H2-DRI-EAF as EU production that will "more than double demand for fossil-free electricity".

So the gap is **not** a deliberate channel for cheap imported green iron. It is the unintended intersection of two separately-motivated choices: (a) exclude indirect emissions where ICC applies, to avoid double protection; and (b) benchmark DRI on direct emissions, tightened in Dec 2025 precisely to stop DRI-based goods owing nothing. Recital 16 of Impl. Reg. 2025/2620 proves the Commission will move fast against a zero-liability outcome for DRI **when it sees one** — it saw the direct-emissions version and legislated within the year. It has not seen the indirect-emissions version.

**The strategic reading:** the gap is *known-in-general*, *accepted-in-general*, *unintended-in-particular*, and — most usefully — *unexamined in exactly the form that matters*. The Commission's stated position, its own contractor's analysis, and the Council's legislative text all converge on a framing (sectoral carve-out + ICC + product-shuffling enforcement) that cannot see the case of a new-build hydrogen-based iron plant abroad on fossil power. That is an open analytical gap with a named owner (DG TAXUD Unit C.2), a live forum (the Expert Group on the CBAM, given a monitoring role in Council recital 12c), and a scheduled hook (the Art. 30(6) report due before 1 January 2028, whose topic list is still being legislated in 2025/0419(COD)).
